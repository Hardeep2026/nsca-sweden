TEENAGE TO YOUNG ADULT

Your care.
Your voice. Your rights.

Simple answers for teenagers and young adults with sickle cell in Sweden. Growing up, school, work, friends, and looking after yourself.

START WITH WHAT'S ON YOUR MIND

Things people your age ask us

Moving to adult care

  • This usually happens in your late teens. Ask your care team when it will happen and how it will work.
  • Ask for one person to contact at the adult clinic. Ask to visit or talk to them before you move.
  • Start doing things yourself: book an appointment, know your medicines, and explain sickle cell in your own words.
  • Make your care plan together with your care team, so it fits what matters to you.
  • Keep a paper copy of your care plan and carry it with you.
  • Bring a parent, partner or friend if you want. It is your choice.

School, work and telling people

  • You decide who to tell, and how much. You do not owe anyone your whole story.
  • Ask for what helps you: water close by, rest breaks, and time off for appointments or hospital.
  • A simple way to say it: “I have sickle cell disease. It is a blood condition. Sometimes I need to rest or go to hospital.”
  • The Discrimination Act ( Diskrimineringslagen) protects you at work and at school. If someone treats you worse because of your condition, or won't give you the help you need, that can be discrimination. Employers and schools must also work to stop it.

Looking after yourself day to day

  • Drink water all day, and more when it is hot or you are active.
  • Keep warm, rest when you are tired, and take your medicines the way your doctor told you.
  • Keep your vaccinations up to date. If you have a fever, get help straight away.
  • Eat well: less sugar and balanced meals help you keep your energy.
  • Find something you love to do that takes your mind off sickle cell.
  • Know your warning signs, and when to call your clinic or go to the emergency room.

WHY THIS PAGE EXISTS

Sickle cell is part of your story, not all of it.

“I was a teenager once, and I know what it is like to feel alone, and to not be able to do what your friends can do. Friendship was what I struggled with most. Then I found a friend with sickle cell. We understood what we were both going through, and it helped more than I can say. Sickle cell is part of your story, not all of it. And if someone doesn’t want to be your friend because of it, they are not meant to be in your corner.”

Toni Forsberg Founder & Chair, NSCA Sweden
